After negotiating some of the trip details, prices, and dates, we've settled on
the following schedule: 5 days for the hike, 2 days for commuting, 1 day for
Kathmandu, all depending on the weather and flights. Since I would be staying
a layover day in Kathmandu anyway, the tour operator offered me a day tour of
the most popular spots in the Nepal capital.

The trip starts off at the hotel, where you are picked up by a dedicated driver
by the tour guide, or you walk out on foot by yourself. My tour guide booked a
hotel room for me in the tourist district, so the route on this page starts
there.

## Swayambhu Mahachaitya

You then get to Swayambhu Mahachaitya, an ancient religious complex atop a hill
in the Kathmandu Valley, west of Kathmandu city. It's surprisingly close-by,
just a few kilometers away from my starting point. That being said, the weather
and humidity don't make the trip easy.

![The view from the top of the complex](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-03.jpg)

Once you reach the complex, you have to walk up some steps, as the temple is
located on a hill. I was told the exact step count, but at the end of the day,
it's not that high.

![Swayambhu Statue of Peace fountain](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-00.jpg)

I suggest you start this visit off by going to the Statue of Peace fountain on
the complex premises. It's a nice area with a drinking water fountain nearby,
plus the place is brimming with monkeys.

![A monkey eating an offering flower](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-04.jpg)
![The Swayambhu Mahachaitya stupa itself](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-01.jpg)

You can then take a look at the Maitreya Buddha Reincarnate shrine or at one of
the nearby stupas (Buddhist temple structures). There's plenty of them to come
on this trip, but each one feels unique and potentially worships a different
deity.

![Harati Ajima Temple installation](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-02.jpg)

From there you can go up to the Swayambhu Mahachaitya stupa and the adjacent
Shantipur Shrine or Harati Ajima Temple. Each one has a different purpose:
people walk clock-wise along the stupa ringing the cyllindric bells for good
fortune, pray at the temple and give offerings at the shrine. There are some
golden circles on the ground that people shouldn't step on if they don't
want to make an act of severe spiritual disrespect.

## Durbar Square

Next up is the Kathmandu Durbar Square, the national heritage site with a
local palace, tons of temples and shrines, and the temple of the
[Living Goddess](https://ntb.gov.np/living-goddess-kumari). There's also the
Basantapur Dabu merchant square selling trinkets and souvenirs to passer-bys.
In general, I found it interesting that despite the large quantities of
international tourists from Europe and America, the biggest tourist group on
average throughout the year is Indian. Kathmandu is a great pilgrimage site in
Hinduism, and India has a no-visa agreement with Nepal making border crossings
accessible to people from different wealth groups, more on that in the
Annapurna hike posts.

Although I wasn't interested in the merchant square, there's also a normal
tourist street with shops, cafes, ATMs, and tourism agencies right next to it.
I had to withdraw some cash for the eventual tourist guide and waiter tips,
so I visited an ATM and paid a 5% local ATM fee on top of the 2% German bank
fee.

![The Kasthamandap temple](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-05.jpg)

The tour of the square in general began with the [Kasthamandap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasthamandap)
landmark, a multi-story wooden temple that was reconstructed multiple times
after numerous earthquakes and fires. I can't say too much about this temple
though. The Kathmandu city took its name from the temple, but apparently it's
not interesting enough to warrant talking about for local guides and tourism
pages, even the official [Nepal government page](https://ntb.gov.np/kathmandu-durbar-square).

![The Kasthamandap temple](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-06.jpg)

Following the crowd along the street, one walks past the Tago Gan Bell and the
King Pratap Malla Column towards the [Shree Kaal Bhairav Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaal_Bhairav,_Kathmandu),
a detailed stone sculpture from a single stone of the Hindu deity Kaal Bhairav,
dating from the 6th century. While I was there, I noticed that the statue was
splashed in all sorts of bright colors, just like the people praying to it.
Usually people walk barefoot with their dogs out inside Hindu temples, but this
one is open-air, so the visitors had mixed opinions on the attire rules. The
only thing I can assure you of is the pavement everyone was walking on was not
clean by any standards.

![The Taleju Bhawani temple](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-18.jpg)

A bit further and to the right is the main Taleju Bhawani Temple, built by
King Mahendra Malla in 1549 AD. Normally the temple is closed (except for one
day during the yearly Dashain festival), and the temple is used to worship the
private deity of the royal family. I found it crazy that the royal family can
just have an exclusive deity just for them that only they are allowed/supposed
to pray to. I wonder if it's stronger and gives them better blessings compared
to common peoples' deities and gods.

![The Kumari temple Living Goddess balcony](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-07.jpg)

Afterwards, I turned around and walk to the Kumari temple, the temple of the
[Living Goddess](https://ntb.gov.np/living-goddess-kumari).
I'm not sure if I was given a "stupid tourist" explanation of what she is and
what she does, but the basic practical idea is the following. Any religious
family from the Shakya Buddhist clan (yes, clans and casts are still a thing
in India, Nepal, and probably some surrounding countries) can submit their
infant to become the vessel for the Kumari Goddess to possess. More on the
deity later. After some selection process, one candidate is chosen and taken
into the temple in Kathmandu. There, the child prays most of the day and
receives a normal-ish common education. Her other duties include showing up
on a balcony in the temple and blessing all the visitors and taking part in
Indra Jatra and other local festivals, where she's taken out and paraded
across the city in her golden palanquin (a covered seat carried on poles on
shoulders). Once the girl hits puberty, she's released back to her family,
loses the divine status, and the selection process starts over. I've been
told that it's hard for these girls to find husbands afterwards, as locals are
afraid of receiving the wrath of Kumari if they mistreat their girlfriend/wife.

From the spiritual side, Kumari represents Taleju Bhawani, and is the living
incarnation of the goddess herself. According to legend, the goddess
incarnation would frequent the palace and entertain the king until she had a
conflict with King Jayaprakash Malla, after which she disappeared. Later on
after tons of prayers she gave the king a vision to choose a young Kumari for
her to possess so that others can continue to worship her. In short, the
goddess Taleju Bhawani (the spiritual abstract goddess at the eternal plane)
incarnates here on mortal Earth as Kumari, and after she possesses a girl, that
girl also becomes a/the Kumari. The purpose, I assume, is that collecting
prayers in-person using an incarnation is more effective than people praying to
the original deity directly?

![The Nasal Chowk courtyard](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-08.jpg)
![The horny statue with the penis out on a Nasal Chowk tower](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-19.jpg)

Anyway, sorry for the long ramble, now comes the quick part. My next stop was
the nearby Nasal Chowk, a lovely courtyard inside the historic royal residence
(which I also partially visited). Not too much interesting about it, the yard
was used for coronations until 2001, and the nearby tower is accessible all the
way to the top. There's a nearby Jagannath temple famous for its erotic wooden
carved figures, although there were some in the towers and throughout the
courtyard too.

![The merchant square from the Nasal Chowk courtyard tower](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-09.jpg)

Skipping other temples and statues I don't have "exciting" facts about, I
entered the [Gaddhi Baithak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaddi_Baithak)
hall that straight-up hit me with a European neoclassical interior. It also has
non-typical crystal chandeliers, ornate pillars, a high decorated ceiling and
some local paintings. It was commissioned in 1908 by the Rana Prime Minister
after he toured Europe (the Rana lineage had powerful ties to the British
Empire). They thought this building would legitimize his family's authority
domestically and to visiting Brits. Apparently, this plan actually worked,
after the hall was used for assemblies and meetings, coronations and national
proclamations, and even state festivals.

![The Gaddhi Baithak interior](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-10.jpg)
![AI-generated Hindu paintings along the way](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-11.jpg)

This concluded my tour of the world heritage square, and I moved on to the
Boudha Stupa in a different part of the city.

## Boudha Stupa

Around 8 km east of downtown Kathmandu is the [Boudha stupa](https://ntb.gov.np/boudha),
the largest stupa in the Kathmandu Valley. Although by law most local temples
are shared between Buddhists and Hinduists, this stupa is the center of
Jimalayan Buddhism in particular. As usual, this is a UNESCO World Heritage
Site with people walking there barefoot.

![The commute to the Boudha Stupa](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-15.jpg)
![The Boudha Stupa from below](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-13.jpg)

The temple with the surrounding area is laid out as a massive mandala, which is
a circular structure (usually a painting of a circle) meant for Annuttarayoga
Tantra meditation practices. In practice, it's a circle visually representing
the core essence of the religious teaching of Pure Land, Enlightened Mind.
The elements of the buildings/statues/pictures of mandala represent the local
deities, and the colors represent different forces of nature and human
qualities.

Why is the mandala shape important? There's tons of art schools/centers that
sell mandala paintings for insane amounts of money. I was coerced into buying
the cheapest one for 40\$, but I've seen people buy the medium-quality 120\$
paintings too.

![The Boudha Stupa up close](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-14.jpg)

To experience the local spirituality I also took my shoes off and did a
clockwise walk inside the temple around the stupa to collect all the goodwill
and blessings, then headed to a local restaurant to finally eat something for
the day.

![The stupa meal](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-12.jpg)

To be honest, I don't remember which specific place I went to, but it was one
of the restaurants on the stupa square, with a rooftop terrace overlooking the
temple. As most local establishments, the meal I ordered had a small rice
serving in the shape of the stupa. I didn't think of it back then, but probably
circling the dish with your fork gives you some goodwill too.

## Pashupatinath Temple

Last but not least, I went to the [Pashupatinath Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupatinath_Temple),
one of the most famous Nepal Hindu temples. This one is dedicated to Pashupati,
a manifestation of Shiva. Again, a world heritage site together with the nearby
temples, ashrams, inscriptions, the Bagmati River banks, etc.

![The panorama of the Pashupatinath temple](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-17.jpg)

There are many legends associated with this temple and with Pashupati in
general, but the spiritual impact of praying there seems to come down to
fulfilling all desires and giving good luck and successful wishes. The entrance
to non-Hindu pilgrims is forbidden, so I only took images from the outside.

What I found interesting is that there was a public-funded homeless shelter
right next to the temple, followed by the burial site on the Bagmati River.
An efficient pipeline in case a person dies there if you ask me.

Visiting the Aarya Ghat burial stairs was a weird experience for me. You just
walk past the Mangala Gauri Temple onto the bridge across the river, and you
see locals dipping dead bodies into the river to purify them, surrounding the
now-dipped body with leaves and sticks on the stairs near the river, then
burning it for 5-6 hours. At the end the remains and leftover bones/ash are
thrown back into the river (in which the new dead bodies are purified).

![The Bagmati river at the Pashupatinath temple](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/kathmandu_day_tour/2026-06-07-16.jpg)

The river is sacred and presumably pure, but somewhere upstream people may
throw trash into it, and downstream people may drink from it, so it's a bit of
a weird situation. I tried not to think about it and not to breather the dead
body fumes for too long, although the local guide was surprised I didn't want
to stay longer and film the funeral process (why would I?) as some do.

After such a diverce experience I was sweaty, thirsty, and tired, so I skipped
the Narayanhiti Palace Museum (the exterior of it, the entrance was closed) and
went straight to the hotel. In the colder seasons, I encourage Kathmandu
visitors to also do a day tour of the city. I'm not sure booking a guide makes
sense though, as they tell you roughly the same number of factoids as I did.
Make sure to plan a route in advance though! Both getting a taxi or booking a
full-day driver are reasonably cheap, so if you don't want to walk, get a car!
