Now that I got introduced to the region on my [Kathmandu Day Tour](/hikes/2026-06-07-kathmandu_day_tour/),
it was time to do what I came for. Namely, go all the way to the Base Camp of
Annapurna, one of the highest mountains in the world.

A long time coming and not a long time planning, I was trying to find a shorter
tour of one of the highest peaks in the world. The trail to Everest base camp
(EBC) was much longer, normally 14 days. I had a week at best before going back
to my university teaching duties, so I took Annapurna base camp (ABC) as my
best option. The [Outfitter Himalaya](https://outfitterhimalaya.com/) tour
guide offered a comfortable 5-day track which we set on, although it seems that
many people opt in for even longer options. I was joined by [Parlad Dharel](https://www.instagram.com/parlad_dharel/),
an experienced local base camp guide.

The trip to Annapurna Base Camp starts off in Samrung, a small village deep in
the mountains, yet still accessible by a jeep. People that want to approach
Annapurna from the north begin the ascent at the Birethanti village instead,
although this route doesn't go to the base camp, instead wrapping around the
mountain.

![View from Samrung towards Annapurna](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-00.jpg)

From Samrung, I took the 280-meter suspension bridge that the government built
5 years ago to allow locals to cross the Kyumrung Khola river easier.

Mules/donkeys take supplies all the way to Sinuwa village (2340 m) and the old
path across the river created a lot of traffic jams and danger, so the
government wanted to help out the locals. Parlad told me that in more
touristy seasons you typically wait for an hour to cross the bridge because of
the high volume of hikers and mules.

In June, however, it was mostly empty. We crossed the bridge easily, with my
guide saying that some tourists get scared of heights and can't cross the
bridge without help.

![Looking back at Samrung from Jhinu Danda](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-01.jpg)

We then walked up to Jhinu Danda, a small village made exclusively of hotels
and restaurants serving local food. It has to be said, though, that the food in
the region is quite scarce due to transportation complexity, so you typically
get some dry food with spicy sauce and pickled vegetables.

Because of the hikers, they also offer western food from time to time, like
toast, pancakes or pizza.

![A view through the forest towards the mountains](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-02.jpg)

The local toilets are also simplistic and don't have toilet paper (it's a
resource that runs out quickly, plus locals prefer only washing themselves), so
if you're in the region, make sure to take some with you.

![One of the Tauling kittens](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-03.jpg)

After having lunch in the village, we walked to Taulung and made a short break
there. The weather was really unforgiving, with 30 degree heat, no clouds and
no wind. You need to bring a lot of water and purification tablets, otherwise
you're pretty screwed.

In June the monsoons cover most of the region. In the morning, the sky gets
completely clear with views far into the distance, but around 16:00 the
humidity accumulates and the downpour starts, lasting until late evening.
Thanks to the rains, the local nature is thriving. Lush forests, waterfalls,
moss and ferns, bushes of all kinds with birds hiding among them. It reminded
me of my trip to Madeira, especially the [Caldeirão Verde hike](/hikes/2025-04-04-caldeirao_verde/).

![Mules carrying supplies around the villages](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-04.jpg)

The whole route so far is just stone steps, with the locals repairing small
cracked sections of them from time to time. Around Taulung we also saw a big
group of mules carrying propane tanks and trash down to Samrung, taking the
load off the shoulders of the local sherpas. As I was told, the mules live in
Chhomrong and don't go past Sinuwa, so the rest of the way to Annapurna Base
Camp the supplies need to be carried on foot.

The rest of the way was quite smooth, as it was easier to walk with clouds
covering the sun. We walked to the entrance of Chhomrong, last the local stupa
shrine, went a bit down to the valley and reached the tea house we were
spending the night at.

![View from a local tea house towards Samrung with an ARNA ad](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-05.jpg)

This is the last location where one can reasonably drink alcohol, so I tried
some local ARNA strong Nepali lager. I'm not a connoisseur of beer, so I can't
tell you about the subtle notes of Buddhist culture in the drink, but it tasted
good to me.

![Final view for the day at the road towards Annapurna](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-06.jpg)
![Compilation of the first day of the Annapurna Base Camp hike](/images/projects/data-viz/hikes/stories/annapurna_bc_1/2026-06-09-07.mp4)

This marks the end of the easy section of the hike, named by a passer-by
Australian hiker as the warm-up. From the next day onwards, the hikes get
longer and tougher.
